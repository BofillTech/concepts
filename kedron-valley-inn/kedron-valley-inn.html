South Woodstock, Vermont

Built the same year Andrew Jackson was elected.

The Kedron Valley Inn's post-and-beam bones haven't moved since 1822. The tavern next door still fills with wood-fired pizza and fiddle music most Wednesdays through Saturdays.

Nearly 200 Years

Two centuries, give or take.

1822

Richard Ransom Jr. builds a store for South Woodstock's gristmill workers — the oldest structure on the property today, now known as the Tavern Building. It becomes the village social center, home to the first post office and, for a time, the bank.

1828

The Main House rises as a Federal-style dwelling for the Ransom family — also run as an inn and tavern from the start. It's the same year Andrew Jackson was elected the seventh president.

1848

National Hall connects the Main House to the Tavern Building — a grand auditorium for dances, debates, and town meetings, lost in the early 1900s. Local history also holds the Inn as a stop on the eastern Underground Railroad.

1960s–85

Vermonter Paul Kendall, an eighth-generation South Woodstock resident, adds the Log Lodge, digs the two-acre swimming pond, and converts the old tavern rooms into guest quarters.

Today

Same foundation, same beams — now paired with modern comfort, a working tavern of its own, and caretakers who consider the upkeep part of the job.

Ransom Tavern

Named for the family that built the building in 1822, the Tavern serves Neapolitan-style pizza, salads, and antipasti out of the same brick walls that once housed South Woodstock's post office and bank.

  • Wood-fired pizza
  • The Ransom Tavern pie — gorgonzola, prosciutto, maple

Eleven Acres

Eleven acres, one spring-fed pond, a lot of quiet.

Mornings start on the porch with a book. Afternoons drift toward the beach and dock on the Inn's own two-acre spring-fed pond, or out onto the 100 miles of trails that thread the Green Mountains around South Woodstock — maintained for hiking, riding, and, come winter, snowmobiling.
