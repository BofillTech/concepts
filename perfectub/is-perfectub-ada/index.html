Is Perfectub ADA?

By Jerry GouldOctober 22, 2025

Choosing the Accessibility That’s Right for You

By Vice President of Marketing and Sales, Jerry Gould

Many years ago, I was helping a very nice couple select the plumbing fixtures for their master bedroom suite. Peter and Janet were excited about designing a luxurious shower with thermostatic valves, rain head, and body sprays. We got it all spec’ed out within their budget, and I wrote up the order.

We had a lot of fun. There were jokes and repartee. We were enjoying the work and each other’s company: we were on a first-name basis very quickly. They were really happy and nice throughout the whole experience. It was so very satisfying. The next day, though, the wife came in by herself with a sad and confused look on her face.

I asked her if there was a problem, and she said something that I had heard often in the past: “I showed our plumber the list of shower parts, and he told me that he didn’t like to install thermostatic valves.”

Back then, there were still a lot of plumbers who were not familiar or comfortable with that kind of installation. Having seen many customers frustrated this way and searching for the right answer, I took a chance on humor:

“Do you shower with your plumber, Janet?”

It worked – her smile returned. She understood that I was really asking her, “Whose home is this, anyway?” Architects, builders, plumbers, and building inspectors all too often act as though it’s their home. I went on to say that I’d be happy to talk her plumber through the installation. She thanked me and had him call me the next day.

Your Home, Your Bath

I’ve been helping homeowners plan and furnish their bathrooms for almost five decades, and I’ve seen so many ways that people lose control of their process and don’t get the things and functionality they want as a result. One of the most common ways that they lose control is through government regulations.

The ADA is Exhibit One. The regulations instituted by the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) were only intended to be used as a uniform standard for public accommodations, commercial, and industrial projects to make them more accessible for people with physical challenges. It does not apply to private homes or apartments. Sadly, it is often used as an excuse to advise against or even prevent homeowners from using things they love or need. Well, here again, I ask, “Whose house is it, anyway?”

This becomes a particular problem for people who may have some physical challenges now or want to be proactive about changes they may experience in the future. Of course, it’s natural for housing professionals to rely on codes and standards to help their clients design a safe and enduring home.

However, when an idea or product comes along that is an aesthetic improvement, an accessibility enhancement, and a life enhancer but does not align exactly with the “accepted way of doing things,” a homeowner is free to make that choice.

The Perfectub

The Perfectub by Brak is a case in point! Perfectubs by Brak may appear to be just another sleekly styled standard alcove bathtub at first glance. However, the Perfectub (available in 60×30” or 60×32” dimensions) has two patented design features that make it a far more accessible, enjoyable, safer, and attractive bathtub than the “standard.”

In either size, the Perfectub has more flat space in the floor than any other alcove tub of that size. They are also much easier to take a stable and safe step in or out of the tub. Try this: measure out 8 inches [VERIFY] on the floor and take a step so that the toe on your back foot and the heel on your front foot are 8 inches apart [VERIFY]. That’s the step needed to get in or out of one of the most popular tubs on the market. Now try it with only 3 inches between the feet [VERIFY].

That’s the Perfectub step. Not only that, but these tubs keep the whole bathroom safer because the patented “undercut” or toe kick in the side of the tub that faces the room serves as a splash guard. Most of the slip and fall accidents in bathrooms actually happen because of wet floors outside the bath or shower- so that’s huge.

The other patented feature is the trim, one and a quarter inch wide, steel-reinforced back wall that provides even more flat space to stand on the floor of the tub. It also gives the appearance that the wall around the tub just continues straight down into the tub. It’s just a much cleaner, more modern look.

The Perfectub is actually a better solution for most people than the ADA “go-to” solutions. Access to Perfectub from a wheelchair is a standard transfer procedure. Our 32-inch tub meets the apron height requirements for an ADA bathroom installation. Fold-down or in-tub seats will make transfer into the tub easy and safe.

Should You Get a Roll-in Shower?

It’s true that ADA considers roll-in showers the gold standard because you can “roll-in” in a wheelchair. But it doesn’t allow for a luxurious soak, and you have to transfer from your chair unless you want to shower in your regular chair and then sit in a wet chair while you try to dry off.

Remember, it’s your home! Don’t let commercial guidelines boss you around.

Are Walk-in Tubs Worth It?

Perfectub is also a much better choice for most people than walk-in tubs that have a door for access. When you are done with your bath in a walk-in tub, you will have to wait in the cool air while the water drains out so you can open the door.

Here are more points to consider in comparison to the ADA favorites:

  • With Perfectub, others in the home will have the ability to take an easy shower with plenty of room to stand and move around.
  • You also retain the versatility of having a tub that can be easily used to bathe children or pets
  • Realtors will tell you a good tub is better for resale value
  • There are other uses for a tub, like storing large quantities of water for an emergency situation or taking an ice bath.
  • Perfectub has far more capacity.

For most people, the best accessibility solution is a Perfectub. They are beautiful and comfortable, reinforce the resale value of your home, and, for most people, are safer than what your architect or designer might pull out of an ADA guideline.
